This case study reflects my experience as the primary designer of the design system on a confidential Deloitte Digital project. Due to NDA restrictions I can't share product visuals or name the platform directly, but I can speak to the system thinking, the cross-functional collaboration, and the design infrastructure I built from the ground up.

We had just shipped the MVP. It was fast, scrappy, and full of promise. As it moved into development the cracks began to show: button states varied, spacing rules were improvised, developers pinged us daily for clarification. Nobody had dropped the ball. We simply hadn't built the shared language yet. That became my mandate.

## When speed outpaces structure

Before the design system existed, our workflow was reactive. Components were added on demand, often duplicated or slightly tweaked without documentation. The Figma file was becoming a maze of mismatched styles. Developers struggled to interpret spacing logic and font usage. QA flagged visual bugs that stemmed from design ambiguity rather than code errors. We were losing cohesion fast.

When you move fast and ship in sprints, consistency is often the first casualty. The team was experienced but the product was growing faster than our process was. By the end of that sprint it was clear the current approach wasn't sustainable. So we made a strategic decision: build the system properly, and build it now.

## Building the backbone

Where do we begin? The answer was an audit. I went through the existing Figma files systematically, mapping components, identifying duplications, and documenting the inconsistencies that were causing the most friction downstream.

The guiding principle, borrowed from Dan Mall, was simple: don't try to include everything. Start with what is already used everywhere. Navigation, headers, tables, forms, the components doing the heavy lifting across every screen. Everything else could follow. I benchmarked against IBM Carbon and Google Material Design, systems built for the same kind of scale.

From there I began building. Buttons in all their variants, form elements, navigation patterns, data tables with typed columns, charts, cards, modals, dashboards. The system grew sprint by sprint, always connected to what the product actually needed.

## What we built

The system grew to cover everything the product needed. Buttons in all their states and variants, form elements, navigation patterns, both top and side, expanded and icon-only. Tables with typed columns. Cards, modals, alerts, empty states. Twenty chart types referenced from Carbon and adapted for the platform. Dashboards combining all of the above. The list was long.

In total the system covered 100+ components across six categories, built entirely in Figma and handed off to Storybook in close collaboration with a developer. My role was to design, document, and oversee the implementation, raising issues, reviewing builds, and ensuring what shipped matched what was designed.

## Where design meets code

Building the system in Figma was one thing. Making it real was another. Once a component was built in Storybook, I reviewed it against the design. I wrote up a list. A selected state used the wrong color. Padding values differed from the spec. Drop shadow spread, blur, color, or opacity didn't match the design. Border thickness and color were off. Icon and title sat misaligned. I sat down with my dev counterpart and walked through the list, component by component. I explained the reasoning behind each decision. We figured out what to hold and what to change.

Every review cycle produced another list. Another conversation. Another round of adjustments. The sprint clock kept running. The pressure never let up.

Some of what I asked for held. Some changed to fit what the codebase could support. I negotiated each component against what the design called for and what the code could carry.

## What changed

Design reviews grew faster and more focused. Developers spent less time interpreting spacing or font choices. QA flagged fewer visual bugs rooted in ambiguity.

The impact was cultural too. The system gave the team a way to talk about design with clarity and shared intent. It created space for better feedback, faster iteration, and more inclusive decision-making. A well maintained system does not just reduce inconsistency, it reduces friction between people.

The system ultimately supported 14 product modules across a single enterprise platform, serving data teams and marketers, from data ingestion through to campaign delivery. For a product of that scope, the design system was the foundation everything else built on.

## Systems are relational

Good systems grow with the people who use them.

Over two years on this product, I produced documentation, components, and a Storybook handoff. In the process, I built trust across disciplines, learned to speak a developer's language, and accepted that some of my design decisions needed to change when they met the reality of code.

I came in as a designer, holding a thread between design and development. I left seeing that thread as something else entirely: a space where both happen at once.
