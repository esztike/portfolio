Travel is often romanticized as freedom and adventure. For many though, it's a source of stress, overstimulation, and emotional fatigue. From crowded airports to unpredictable schedules, the journey can feel more like survival than exploration.

Globo began as a question: _What if a travel app could soothe, not just organize?_

As a UX Designer and Researcher, I set out to explore how design could support travelers emotionally, not just logistically. This concept blends user empathy, emotional mapping, and AI-informed personalization to imagine travel that feels more human.

## Travel, a fundamental human experience

Travel should feel good. Neurologically, it often does: new environments trigger dopamine, exposure to different cultures builds cognitive flexibility, novelty disrupts routine in ways that improve problem-solving. The research on this is consistent.

And yet travel is also one of the most reliably stressful experiences people put themselves through voluntarily. Language barriers, logistical complexity, unfamiliar systems, financial pressure, loss of control. The same person who books a trip for the mental health benefits will spend the week before it anxious about packing.

## What makes travel hard

Not speaking the local language can stop people from traveling entirely. Planning a bucket list birthday trip, Ben quickly realized one of his relatives had clear criteria for leaving home. A resort by the ocean, within a six-hour flight, and English-speaking. For this relative, not speaking the language means losing control. The ability to navigate, to ask for help, to understand what's happening around. Without that thread of connection, even a vacation can feel more overwhelming than restful.

For first-time travelers, the unknowns multiply fast. A dear relative of mine traveled for the first time with her entire family, two adults, two young kids, from Hungary to Hawai'i. The distance alone is significant. Add jet lag, sensory overload, unfamiliar airports, transfer connections, and the sheer logistics of moving a family across the world, and it took her almost two weeks to recover.

What would have helped wasn't hand-holding. It was information. Airport maps. Transfer timing. Security estimates. Real-time itinerary updates. Luggage tracking. Food options that accommodated allergies. The fewer the unknowns, the more in control she could feel, and the more she could actually enjoy the trip she'd planned.

Twelve stressors emerged from research, clustered into four categories: communication barriers, logistical unpredictability, environmental overwhelm, and loss of control. The pattern connecting them wasn't random. Most shared a common root: uncertainty. The less predictable a situation, the more anxious the traveler.

## Addressing travel anxiety

Through my interviews, a theme emerged consistently: for many travelers, the emotional weight of a trip begins long before they leave home.

Travel anxiety, the fear or worry that can accompany planning and traveling, shows up differently for everyone. Some feel it as restlessness the night before a flight. Others need years to work up to booking an international trip at all. It's estimated that approximately 25% of travelers experience some form of anxiety before leaving home. As a UX professional, not a mental health expert, I approached these conversations with empathy and without judgment. What people shared with me felt like a privilege, and a clear opportunity to design with more care and inclusivity.

Dora frequently experienced panic attacks that significantly impacted her commute to work. One day, while on a tram, she had a panic attack and called her office in tears, unable to work. From that point on, she avoided public transport and relied on taxis or her husband for commuting. Her fear extended beyond public transport: she couldn't take planes or trains. Visiting relatives in nearby regions required weeks of mental preparation.

With professional help, she developed strategies to manage her fears. Over time, these helped Dora feel more at ease with the idea of traveling. She started with local trips and gradually worked her way up to longer journeys. Eventually, she even managed to take a flight, something she had previously thought impossible.

What worked for her points directly toward design opportunities. Breathing and mindfulness techniques for in-the-moment calm. Visualization and mental rehearsal to reduce anticipatory anxiety. Meticulous planning and backup strategies to restore a sense of control. Gradual exposure: starting small, building confidence over time.

These weren't just Dora's workarounds. They became the foundation for the feature thinking that followed.

## Coping strategies as design opportunities

The leap from research to design wasn't a feature list: it was a mapping exercise. Every stressor pointed toward a response. Every coping behavior pointed toward an opportunity.

Language barriers: real-time translation and cultural context, delivered before and during the trip. Sensory overload: step-by-step wayfinding with timing, not just directions. Panic and loss of control: breathing tools and grounding exercises built into the journey flow, not buried in a settings menu. Anticipatory anxiety: visualization tools and mental preparation features that mirror what Dora already did for herself.

Seven feature categories emerged. The research did its job: it showed exactly where design could make travel feel more human.

## Reflection

Not everyone can simply overcome their fears. Travel anxiety isn't a mindset problem with a technology solution, and designing as if it were would miss the point entirely.

What this research showed is that people already know themselves better than any app does. The travelers who cope well have built their own systems: preparation rituals, breathing techniques, mental rehearsal. They didn't need an app to tell them what they needed. They figured it out.

The role of good design isn't to replace that self-knowledge. It's to support it. To build tools that meet people where they are, reduce the unknowns that fuel anxiety, and get out of the way. The tools we have today are a start and they perhaps can be better.
