During my time at Deloitte Digital, I was part of a small, three-person design team. Our lead designer focused on stakeholder alignment and strategic framing. I worked alongside a senior designer, each of us owning different user flows across releases, while I also built the design system that unified the product experience across modules.

While product specifics remain confidential, this case study shares how I mapped workflows, resolved edge cases, and built the infrastructure to support clarity across roles and modules. A behind-the-scenes look at designing for scale, layer by layer, flow by flow.

## A real problem, built from scratch

Joining a greenfield product at Deloitte felt like exactly the kind of challenge I wanted. A new platform, a real problem, a team building something from scratch. The ambition was significant and so was the complexity underneath it.

We were designing a product that was still defining itself. The brief was clear in direction but open in detail, and that gap between vision and definition was where most of the real design work lived.

The product was a marketing SaaS platform built from scratch. Integrating customer data, enabling smart segmentation, orchestrating multi-channel campaigns, and surfacing performance analytics within a single, scalable experience. Nothing else at the time integrated the full marketing journey, from data processing to campaigning, end to end.

It needed to serve marketers, analysts, IT support, and client teams, each with distinct workflows, priorities, and visibility needs.

## Building sprint by sprint

The work started with user flows. Each sprint had defined scope and clear goals: what this module needed to do, who it served, and what success looked like for that release. We worked in two-week cycles, designing flows before screens, making sure the logic held before the interface took shape.

The product grew incrementally. What started as core data and audience flows expanded sprint by sprint: decisioning models, orchestration, destinations, client configuration, alerts, email deliverability. Fourteen modules in total by the time the platform was complete.

Product owners didn't always have naming decisions ready when we needed them. We kept designing, using working titles or placeholders close enough to the intended meaning. When the final name landed, everything updated with it across the file. It was part of the rhythm.

## Designing inside ambiguity

In projects like this, one of the real challenges is that the product is still being defined while you are designing it.

Module boundaries shifted. Naming wasn't settled. The navigation was where this tension showed up most clearly: it had to hold a coherent structure for users while absorbing a product scope that kept expanding.

From the start the navigation was always visible. A persistent sidebar gave users a clear sense of where they were at all times. As the platform grew and dashboards were introduced, screen real estate became a constraint. We introduced an icon-only collapsed state, giving users more room to work while keeping navigation accessible.

Users of this platform already had existing tools and workflows. The navigation had to feel familiar enough to adopt while fitting the new product's logic. We ran click tests with navigation variants, keeping decisions grounded in how users actually moved through the interface.

Flows were built around user goals rather than product structure. Progressive disclosure meant users only saw what was relevant at each step. Role-aware visibility meant each user type saw the interface shaped around their actual access and responsibilities.

The goal was always the same: keep the user's clarity as the constant when everything else was moving.

## Working it out together

We worked in daily standups. Everyone knew what the other was working on: design, engineering, product. Before the design system existed, reviewing builds in sandbox was constant work. Spacing and padding rarely matched the designs on the first pass. The process itself was still being figured out alongside the product.

It was hectic at times. But the team approached it with honesty and mutual respect, because everyone was dealing with the same challenges. When the design system came together it made a tangible difference, not just for design, but for everyone trying to build consistently across a product of this scale.

## When the backend caught up

The backend evolved in parallel with the design work and brought its own challenges. Early on, certain flows had to be designed around what wasn't yet technically possible. As the backend matured and constraints lifted, a new layer of design work opened up.

Empty states. Error states. Authentication flows. Role-based profiles. Client configuration, because the platform was used internally by Deloitte teams who needed to give their clients a specialised view into the system. Getting these states right mattered as much as the primary flows. A complex product earns trust not just through its main experience but through how it handles the edges.

## What the platform became

The platform grew to support 14 modules, serving data teams and marketers from data ingestion through to campaign delivery.

## Reflection

I came into this project excited. The challenges revealed themselves as the product grew, and so did what I was capable of handling.

Designing inside a product that is still becoming itself, at pace, as part of a team, is a different kind of work. You make the best call you can with what you have. You adapt when things shift. You keep moving even when decisions haven't landed yet.

What I took from it is that ambiguity is not the opposite of good design. It is the condition most design actually happens in. Learning to work well inside it: that is the skill.
